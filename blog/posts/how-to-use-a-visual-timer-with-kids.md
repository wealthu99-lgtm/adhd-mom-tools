## Why visual timers work for kids

"Five more minutes" means nothing to a young child. A shrinking shape shows it. It also changes the dynamic: the timer is the one counting down, not you, which can mean fewer reminders and fewer arguments. Many parents of kids with ADHD find this especially useful, though results vary from child to child.

## How to introduce it

- Practise on something fun first, like building a tower before the timer ends.
- Let your child press start.
- Talk about the colours: "When it turns orange, you have a little time left."
- Praise them for finishing, whether or not they beat the timer.

## Timer ideas by routine

- **Getting dressed:** 10 minutes, with a quick-start timer on the [kids timer](/visual-timer-for-kids/).
- **Tidy-up:** 5 to 10 minutes. Name one area to make it clear.
- **Brushing teeth:** 2 minutes.
- **Homework:** 10 to 15 minutes of work, then a short break.
- **Eating:** 15 to 20 minutes so meals do not drag on.
- **Screen time ending:** set a timer before the show ends so the stop is not a surprise.
- **Bedtime wind-down:** a 20-minute countdown through pyjamas, teeth and a book.

## Tips that help

1. Give a warning at two minutes.
2. Keep the tone friendly. It is a game, not a threat.
3. Do not use the timer as punishment.
4. Expect some testing for the first few days.
5. Use a full-screen view on a tablet so it is easy to see across the room.

## For children with ADHD

Break tasks into smaller chunks, add a short reward after a finished timer, and pair the timer with a simple checklist.
